# Reshape Trenchbore around surprise and real rewards

The best-loved games in this group are **short (2 to 5 hours), cheap ($3 to $5), active, and they have a real ending**. Nodebuster has **97.4% positive reviews from 14,072 reviews at $2.99** ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3107330/)). Digseum has **97.7% from 8,164 reviews at $2.99** ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3361470/)). Players love four things: power they can see and feel, goals that pay something real, new screens that appear only when needed, and a game that keeps changing as you go. Players complain most about the **second half**. Either the game "plays itself", or costs jump into a grind wall, or the deeper zones look and play the same as the first ones. These games do not build suspense by showing things slowly. They build it by **hiding the goal, teasing the next thing with a clue or a sound, and changing the rules of the game every so often**. On money, a $3 game sold only on itch.io will most likely earn **tens to low hundreds of dollars**. The $3 games that earned real money did it on Steam. The verdict for Trenchbore is **reshape, do not scrap**. Its skeleton (short dives, a tree between dives, 9 zones, bosses, an ending) is the same skeleton the hits use. The things you dislike are surface problems that the research also says to fix. The real work is deeper: make every zone new, make drilling feel heavy, and make the last hour a climax, not a grind.

A note on the evidence. The research team pulled Steam review numbers on 26 September 2026 and read the most helpful positive and negative reviews for each game. Reddit could not be reached, so views from r/incremental_games come from other places where the same players talk, such as a ResetEra thread on "bite-sized incremental games" ([ResetEra](https://www.resetera.com/threads/bite-sized-incremental-games.1087230/)) and Steam forums. One game from your list, "Loot Blasters", could not be found on Steam at all; searches returned Loot Loop, BORE BLASTERS and Loot Frog instead ([Steam search](https://store.steampowered.com/api/storesearch/?term=Loot%20Blasters&cc=us); [Top Incremental Games](https://www.topincrementalgames.com/tag/skill-tree/steam)). Some small UI details, such as where Nodebuster puts its tooltip, could not be confirmed from any text source. Where a point is my own suggestion and not from a source, I say so. I have not played Trenchbore; the verdict is based on your description of it.

### What players praise

Players praise **short, honest value**. One Nodebuster review says: "For $3, this game is everything you want it to be… It's 3-4 hours of upgrading the power of your auto-shooting mouse pointer until it blaps everything instantaneously on the screen" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197970387498/recommended/3107330/)). A Digseum review says: "not every incremental has to be a multi-month slog" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198041398412/recommended/3361470/)). Another player wrote: "I am starting to really fall in love with these short designed experiences as opposed to the neverending incrementals" ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3361470/discussions/0/596260925367959438)). A press review of Nodebuster agreed: "I was done with Nodebuster in just over four hours. But, contrary to what people might expect, that's what makes it so fun" ([The Mancunion](https://mancunion.com/2025/05/08/nodebuster-review-a-short-and-sweet-incremental-game/)). Another wrote: "Having small games like this is crucial in these days full of infinite games" ([Lyca review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198185692370/recommended/3421300/)). On ResetEra, players called Nodebuster an "intense but short incremental game, lasting 3-4 hours", valued "an actual ending", and said games "need more interaction, otherwise I can go make lunch and finish the game" ([ResetEra](https://www.resetera.com/threads/bite-sized-incremental-games.1087230/)). Players now use the word **"shortcremental"** for this kind of game ([Lyca review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198194640009/recommended/3421300/); [Piñata Go Boom review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198194640009/recommended/3269180/)). They also enjoy a clean 100% finish: "Neat little incremental upgrade game, can finish it 100% in a few hours" ([Row Divers review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198026321547/recommended/2572300/)), and "after I got them all, i dug out the whole backyard… truly a religious experience" ([A Game About Digging A Hole review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198084735507/recommended/3244220/)). The top positive review of that game (1,065 helpful) asks for more after the end: "If they add an endless mode, that's it. It's over" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198036869807/recommended/3244220/)). In a rough word count over 1,500 top positive reviews of 15 short games, price and value words appeared in about 30%, length words in about 26%, and "satisfying" or sound words in about 17% ([Steam review sample](https://store.steampowered.com/appreviews/3107330?json=1&language=english&filter=all)). This count is crude, because "short" can be praise or complaint.

Players praise **power they can see**. A Keep on Mining! player wrote: "When I got lighting, I was like, 'Oh yeah!' It felt good and I got excited" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198148347811/recommended/3769130/)). Others love "big numbers and absurd amounts of particles flying around the screen" ([To The Core review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198064410692/recommended/1988550/)), "haha big numbers go bRRRRRR… Destroying the planets is satisfying" ([To The Core review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198856787389/recommended/1988550/)), and sound: "The euphony of different sound effects is very satisfying toward the end" ([Magic Archery review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199556109102/recommended/2905170/)). They love big talent trees: "the unlocking system is a massive mine in itself" ([Keep on Mining! review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198034831659/recommended/3769130/)).

Players praise **little waiting**. "You don't need to wait for more than 10-30 seconds at any point in the game. In my opinion this is the best way this kind of games should be designed" ([Magic Archery review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198015261565/recommended/2905170/)). Press liked that Nodebuster runs "require active input early on, then gradually ease into a more idle-friendly pace" and called it "all-killer-no-filler" ([The Mancunion](https://mancunion.com/2025/05/08/nodebuster-review-a-short-and-sweet-incremental-game/)). Another press review said: "This is an active experience, not a passive idle one" ([Higher Plain Games](https://higherplaingames.com/pc/nodebuster-review/)). The phrase "one more run" comes up again and again ([Super Mining Mechs review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198050048216/recommended/2830150/); [holedown review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198021192931/recommended/1268370/); [BORE BLASTERS review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197968339199/recommended/2398170/); [Loot Loop review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197960275654/recommended/3972320/)). Only a few reviews talk about the first minutes directly, but one warns: "If you don't like it within the first 5 minutes, it's not going to change your mind" ([Bills Must Be Paid review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198025195084/recommended/4421010/)).

For a drilling game, **nostalgia for the old Flash game Motherload is a free hook**. The top-voted A Game About Digging A Hole review (538 helpful votes) says: "Been yearning for a game like since the flash game Motherload on Miniclip back in the day" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198022691143/recommended/3244220/)). Others say "reminds me a bit of the old Armor Games flash incremental games" ([Nodebuster review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197975640169/recommended/3107330/)) and "It's Motherload but no health or fuel so it's actually relaxing" ([Mining Mechs review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198102107891/recommended/1603180/)). YouTube matters too. Several top reviews say they found the game through the YouTuber "Real Civil Engineer", including the #1 Nodebuster review ([Nodebuster review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198098254030/recommended/3107330/); [Digseum review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198025453137/recommended/3361470/); [Magic Archery review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198055075212/recommended/2905170/)).

### What players complain about

The biggest complaint is the **second-half collapse**. In Nodebuster, "the last 45 minutes essentially plays itself" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198142781697/recommended/3107330/)). Another player wrote: "You quite literally don't have to do ANYthing for the last hour except click play, click the only upgrade left in the linear tree, and click play again" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198267996279/recommended/3107330/)). A third said that "somewhere between a third and halfway through the game, you snowball in strength enough that moving the mouse is no longer required" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198024942939/recommended/3107330/)). Press agreed, calling the level progression "very lopsided" and noting "You have to unlock everything anyway which takes away some of the skill factor" ([Higher Plain Games](https://higherplaingames.com/pc/nodebuster-review/)). Keep on Mining! has the opposite problem: "all the requirements for upgrades get absurdly high and do basically nothing" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197998051618/recommended/3769130/)). It is "satisfying for… 2/3 of its runtime after which it turns overly grindy" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198041057648/recommended/3769130/)). A Piñata Go Boom player said "The vast majority of upgrades feel intangible and cost way too much" and asked for "a 2.5 - 3 hour experience" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198060506002/recommended/3269180/)). BORE BLASTERS is "Very satisfying game from start to … half" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198008332774/recommended/2398170/)); "after an hour or 3 maybe 4 there's no more meta progression and whats left is grinding" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198086868036/recommended/2398170/)), and there is "a lot more playtime than there is content" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197996356022/recommended/2398170/)).

The next complaint is **no real choices**. The top negative Keep on Mining! review (134 helpful) says: "There's no builds that change the way you play, there's no strategy. You spin your mouse in a circle for 30 seconds then buy upgrades and repeat" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198044935812/recommended/3769130/)). Others say "The tech tree was almost all repeats of just 'more damage, more speed'" ([Deep Space Cache review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197984746928/recommended/3226750/)) and "The tech tree is incredibly simple with no real choices" ([Digseum review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197966052070/recommended/3361470/)). Feed the Reactor is called "Extremely linear, false illusion of choice, almost 0 strategy… click, wait, click, wait" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197962770775/recommended/4009730/)). In Super Mining Mechs, "each part is always strictly better than the previous one, so there is no reason to pick an older part" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198111706890/recommended/2830150/)). In Coin Factory, "an exponential growth node… trivializes every strategy" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198006923068/recommended/2992500/)).

Players also complain about **sameness**. Of Row Divers: "The first 5 seconds of your gameplay will contain the entirety of what you will see in the game" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198120222580/recommended/2572300/)), and "the trailer show[s] everything that is in the game" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199074593713/recommended/2572300/)). Of Super Motherload: "The digging feels weightless, blocks simply disappear with no animation. There are no novelty ores in the different layers of rock" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198025820005/recommended/269110/)), while "In the original, the deeper you go, the more interesting it gets" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198050370440/recommended/269110/)). The most helpful negative Super Motherload review has 1,044 helpful votes ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197983942864/recommended/269110/)). Row Divers also has "basically two types of monsters (ranged/melee), just reskins" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197984761823/recommended/2572300/)). Even Ball x Pit's levels "lack interesting enemy patterns and behaviours, gameplay curveballs/surprises" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197968436608/recommended/2062430/)).

Mining games have their own special complaints. In Mining Mechs, "the digging, it's extremely slow, Even when you max out your drill" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198035826989/recommended/1603180/)) and "You spend half of your time traveling back to the surface" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198811777201/recommended/1603180/)). Idle income can beat real play, which feels wrong: "the afk mine brings in more money than mining for ore" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198047787040/recommended/1603180/)) and "AFKing is better than playing" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197960457558/recommended/1603180/)). The reverse also happens: Keep on Mining!'s passive mine "is simply never worth investing a single penny" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198044916562/recommended/3769130/)). Super Motherload's boss is "boring and unfair, and if you die then you lose the money you spent trying to fight, and must go all the way down the mineshaft to rebattle" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198073706057/recommended/269110/)). Its last hours have "Tons of rocks that need bombs to break them up, magma blocks that hurt you to drill through" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198025056840/recommended/269110/)). But zero danger is also a problem: "you *literally cannot lose in this game.* There is no risk or danger" ([Super Mining Mechs review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198070635737/recommended/2830150/)). And if players can skip the whole game, they will: one wished the developers had "prevented players to dig straight down and beat the game in under an hour" ([Keep Digging review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198199518861/recommended/3585800/)).

Endings get criticism too. "The ending is really the let down" ([Nodebuster review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198006575550/recommended/3107330/)). "Anti climatic ending as well" ([Digseum review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198444744885/recommended/3361470/)). "The ending is an infuriating slog… money becomes functionally useless once you've gotten the upgrades" ([A Game About Digging A Hole review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198321515544/recommended/3244220/)). Some games feel too short: "we dug found ending refunded since we beat game in like 1 hour" (123 helpful) ([Keep Digging review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199792768162/recommended/3585800/)). Others: "hoping for an eternal dig to the centre of the earth. Turns out the game only has 2 hours… ends quickly and abruptly" ([A Game About Digging A Hole review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198064509919/recommended/3244220/)); "After your first prestige you think 'nice, now comes the crazy scaling' but then it justs ends" ([Loot Loop review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198017528170/recommended/3972320/)); "Feels like a demo, rather than a full game" ([Keep on Mining! review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198042449873/recommended/3769130/)); and "Its like they make them just long enough for no refunds" ([Bills Must Be Paid review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198068286142/recommended/4421010/)).

Players dislike **resets that repeat content**. "forces you to do five full prestiges, each one rebooting you back to square one with only slight changes" ([Max Manos review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198194640009/recommended/3428620/)). But a reset that brings new play is praised: "It is so cool to have new game play every ascension" ([Max Manos review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199811288100/recommended/3428620/)). ("Prestige" or "ascension" means you reset your progress to get a permanent bonus.) Other players complain: "after 4th map you are forced to prestige every other map… cheap way of duplicating its content" ([Digseum review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198125577857/recommended/3361470/)); "Needing to go back to a previous prestige in order to progress faster was not fun" ([Nodebuster review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198864970315/recommended/3107330/)); Loot Loop makes you "repeat the same 6 scenarios, but even faster" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198802485882/recommended/3972320/)). On ResetEra, one player said of Digseum: "I don't like its focus on restarting progress over and over" ([ResetEra](https://www.resetera.com/threads/bite-sized-incremental-games.1087230/)).

Players also get lost when the game does not explain itself. "It's unclear what the best way is to make the multipliers go up" ([Feed the Reactor review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197969216248/recommended/4009730/)). Nodebuster has a "Lack of visual indicators for many effects in the game, such as crits, auto-collect" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198029402002/recommended/3107330/)). In the Gnorp Apologue, "they don't even share enough detail for you to accurately minmax" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198093281104/recommended/1473350/)).

Some complaints are about **health and comfort**. Players ask for small comforts: "toggle dig on and off rather than having to hold button down" (538 helpful) ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198022691143/recommended/3244220/)) and "I'd like an option to skip the results screens after each dig" (52 helpful) ([Digseum review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198144574254/recommended/3361470/)). To The Core "makes you very dizzy even with screenshake turned off" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198080212341/recommended/1988550/)). Max Manos gives "Great sensory feedback at first, but quickly turns into an aimless, overstimulating loop" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197981133643/recommended/3428620/)). Slowdowns and crashes also cost reviews ([Lyca review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198012498116/recommended/3421300/); [To The Core review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198068927562/recommended/1988550/); [BORE BLASTERS review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198008332774/recommended/2398170/); [Keep Digging review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198042361106/recommended/3585800/)).

Could be the best game in the world and I'd downvote over this" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197964008906/recommended/2830150/)), and "The first screen… just looks like a mobile ad" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198019121917/recommended/2830150/)). Idle Slayer lost goodwill when the developer "slowed the game's speed down by over 50%" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198032227212/recommended/1353300/)); another review says "Idle Slayer has slain it's IDLE component" (127 helpful) ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198037384323/recommended/1353300/)). A Gnorp fan attacked a mobile copy "filled with microtransactions" and "scummy mobile predation tactics" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198425089937/recommended/1473350/)). Long idle games also scare some players: "it seems like doing 80-90% completion will easily take over 300h" ([Idle Slayer review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198059632428/recommended/1353300/)). Long free idle games also get "too slow" complaints: "It's not even a clicker-idle, it's just idle while you wait" ([Mr.Mine review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198075324976/recommended/1397920/)).

### What separates a 95% game from an 80% game

The top games' main complaint is "I wish there was more". A mixed Nodebuster review still recommends it: "Good game for the price, finished in 4 and a half hours. Wish there was more to do afterwards though" (94 helpful) ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198028936322/recommended/3107330/)). Lower-rated games add friction: too many currencies ([Lyca review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198017526014/recommended/3421300/)), unclear goals ("never really understood why I should care", [Coin Factory review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198084597763/recommended/2992500/)), or a price above the hours ("This would be a perfect $3", said of the $10 holedown, [review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198007748381/recommended/1268370/); "If it was 1 or 2 euro's, easy recommend", said of Feed the Reactor at 6.89€ for 4 hours, [review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197969216248/recommended/4009730/); "worth $5 at most as i finished the game in under 6 hours", said of To The Core, [review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198035472623/recommended/1988550/)). Free games get more forgiveness. Players of the free Magic Archery even begged for a price: "This sucker deserves more work, more depth and, in the end, a price tag above $0" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197999394473/recommended/2905170/)). Wrong store tags also cause bad reviews: "this is not an idler game, it's an incremental game… sets up different people to expect different things" ([Feed the Reactor review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197966821516/recommended/4009730/)); "Idler-tag is misplaced" ([Max Manos review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197961577117/recommended/3428620/)); "Reviews on Steam and Youtube tricked me into thinking this is a simulator game" (147 helpful) ([A Game About Digging A Hole review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198086401425/recommended/3244220/)). A developer who answers players helps a lot: "Developer responds immediately and a patch is thrown up within an hour" (174 helpful) ([Super Motherload review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198013208890/recommended/269110/)), and after updates, a Deep Space Cache player wrote "I didn't think the game could be improved but the dev did it somehow" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198042146105/recommended/3226750/)). Silence is punished: "the dev apparently not replying to a single comment in the forums" ([Row Divers review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197984761823/recommended/2572300/)); "0 updates, game is abandoned" ([To The Core review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198035472623/recommended/1988550/)). Price alone does not sink a game. Bills Must Be Paid holds 96.7% at $6.99 because it "delivers very high intensity fun for about ~3-5 hours… It's not just a slop game, it actually has substance" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198369499700/recommended/4421010/)).

The bar is rising. Players are tired of copies: "Short, nothing special, just like the 6 other incrementals that come out every day" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198068286142/recommended/4421010/)), "I've played a lot of the node-likes and this is easily one of the most 'mid' of them… One of the places where these games can shine is where you can make decisions" ([Loot Loop review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198045373190/recommended/3972320/)), and "just another nodelike derivative" ([Lyca review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198066877580/recommended/3421300/)). Even Nodebuster is "oversold compared to the worship it's been getting on reddit" for one player ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198017862044/recommended/3107330/)). One Row Divers review simply says "AI slop game" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197996776320/recommended/2572300/)). A game in this space now needs something players have not seen.

## Every goal should pay something real, or it should not be shown

The best games in this genre never show a goal that pays nothing. **The goal and the reward are the same thing.**

Nodebuster has a "Milestones" tab. It rewards you "with bits or nodes for reaching a certain threshold of enemies defeated" ([The Mancunion](https://mancunion.com/2025/05/08/nodebuster-review-a-short-and-sweet-incremental-game/)). The rewards are currency, not badges. The tab itself is unlocked by buying a node in the tree ([Nodebuster Bible guide](https://www.mejoress.com/the-nodebuster-bible-complete-walkthrough-skill-tree-and-prestige-strategy/)). But Nodebuster also shows a mistake: "the 'Milestones' aren't calibrated to the content length -- I finished the game with three still incomplete despite the entire upgrade tree being long since finished" ([incrementaldb review](https://www.incrementaldb.com/community/review/5808)). A goal that lasts longer than the game feels pointless.

Vampire Survivors, a close cousin of this genre, goes further. Its achievements are called "Unlocks", and each one gives new content: items, characters, stages or gold ([Vampire Survivors Wiki](https://vampire.survivors.wiki/w/Achievements)). Reach level 5 and you get the Wings item. Survive 20 minutes and you get the Pentagram weapon. As one guide puts it, "achievements ARE the unlock system" ([Rogue Ranker](https://rogueranker.com/vampire-survivors-achievements/)).

Idle Slayer uses "Quests". You unlock the Quests tab by buying an upgrade ([Idle Slayer Wiki](https://idleslayer.fandom.com/wiki/Quests)). Each Daily Quest pays a Dragon Egg and each Weekly Quest pays a Simurgh Egg, which are crafting materials ([Daily Quests](https://idleslayer.fandom.com/wiki/Daily_Quests); [Weekly Quests](https://idleslayer.fandom.com/wiki/Weekly_Quests)). You must "claim" a finished quest, which makes a small reward moment. Steam achievements only copy the in-game quests ([Steam guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2981185240)).

Mr. Mine ties goals to **depth**, which fits Trenchbore well. It has "90+ quests to complete… complete them to earn valuable rewards", and reaching 300 km "automatically unlock[s] another 15 quests" ([Mr. Mine blog](https://blog.mrmine.com/mr-mine-idle-underground-city-the-complete-guide/)). Each depth milestone opens a whole new system ([Mr. Mine Milestones blog](https://blog.mrmine.com/mr-mine-idle-milestones/)):

| Depth | What opens |
|---|---|
| 10 km | Super Miners |
| 15 km | Trading Post |
| 45 km | Cave Building (drones) |
| 100 km | Chest Collector |
| 300 km | Underground City |
| 501 km | The Core (sacrifice relics for rewards) |
| 1,000 km | The Moon (a second world) |

Ball x Pit uses "Blueprints". After a boss, a blueprint may drop. You pick it up to unlock a building for good. The level-select screen shows how many blueprints are still hidden in each level ([Ball x Pit wiki](https://ballxpit.wiki.gg/wiki/Blueprints)). That is a goal you can count and find.

Players are clear about empty achievements. On Steam forums, they call them "just a small colored picture", "useless" and "functionally worthless and not worth the time and effort". They say achievements "should be more rewarding or count to something bigger" ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/discussions/forum/10/3004424478068764745); [thread 2](https://steamcommunity.com/discussions/forum/10/3722818378189073388)).

**None of the sources use the word "contracts".** The common names are Milestones, Quests, Unlocks and Blueprints. For a submarine, names like "Orders", "Dispatches", "Survey Tasks" or "Salvage Requests" would fit. These themed names are my suggestion, not from a source. The rule for Trenchbore is simple. Every goal names its reward on its face: currency, a new module, a relic slot, a shortcut to a deeper zone, or a logbook page. A player clicks to claim it. The list of goals must run out before the game does. If you keep Steam achievements later, they should only copy these in-game goals. Your dislike of empty achievements matches what players say.

## Show damage on the rock, and save big health bars for bosses

In this genre, small targets usually **do not have a health bar that is always visible**. Games use other ways to show damage.

Holedown puts a **number on each block**. It shows how many hits the block needs, and it "can easily be in the hundreds" ([TapSmart](https://www.tapsmart.com/games/review-holedown-ball-bouncing-block-breaking-blast/); [Pocket Gamer](https://www.pocketgamer.com/holedown/review/)). The number is both the health bar and a clue for strategy. In Dome Keeper, "it takes several hits for your drill to break apart rock when starting a run". The feedback for an upgrade is simply faster digging, which "feels great and builds up with each upgrade purchased" ([Josh Anthony, Design Dive](https://joshanthony.info/2023/05/24/design-dive-dome-keeper/)). Its art is "crisp, and easy to read", with "small particles" and a "satisfying _shoom_ sound" (same source). In A Game About Digging A Hole, each early shovel hit "hardly leav[es] a dent in the ground", and "the speed quickly picks up as you gain upgrades" ([The Xbox Hub](https://www.thexboxhub.com/a-game-about-digging-a-hole-review/)). The hole itself is the damage display.

Vampire Survivors lets players **turn off damage numbers**, because "enemies die so quickly that damage numbers are meaningless, and the screen is already busy" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1794680/discussions/0/3470612993483101082/); [toggle request](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1794680/discussions/0/663863457493310106/)). According to a modding page, its enemy health bars "disappear if you don't deal damage to them for at least three seconds" ([HealthBarViewer](https://github.com/6thmoon/HealthBarViewer)). So bars appear only on enemies you just hit.

**Bosses are different.** Players need to read boss health. In Void Scrappers, upgrades are written in boss health, such as "automatically killing a boss when it comes to x%" ([Missi the Achievement Huntress](https://www.missitheachievementhuntress.com/void-scrappers-review/)). In Nodebuster, the one bar that matters most is **your own health**: a virus "drains your health faster and faster the longer you stay in a wave", and this "is your biggest blocker to progression" ([Higher Plain Games](https://higherplaingames.com/pc/nodebuster-review/)).

There is a balance to find between **"juice"** (screen shake, particles, sound) and clarity. A famous 2012 talk, "Juice It or Lose It", showed how much juice adds to a simple game ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1016487/juice-it-or-lose); [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fy0aCDmgnxg)). Another designer warned that "There has been such a tremendous focus on putting eye candy in our games… that the context doesn't get enough consideration" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-indies-resist-the-urge-to-juice-it-or-lose-it-)). The reviews in the first section show both sides: weightless digging is punished, and too much flashing is punished too.

For Trenchbore, the research suggests this. Show damage on the rock itself: cracks that grow, a short white flash on each hit, and debris when it breaks. (The exact number of crack stages, for example three, is my suggestion, not from a source.) If a small target needs a bar, show a tiny one only after the first hit and let it fade after about 3 seconds. Use a hit-count number only on special tough blocks where the number helps you plan. Give each boss **one large bar with marks for its phases**. Make damage numbers optional, or show them only for big hits. Add a flashing and screen-shake setting that **really works**. And one small lesson from Nodebuster: "killing the boss instantly ends the level but does not pick up any uncollected resources" ([incrementaldb review](https://www.incrementaldb.com/community/review/5808)). If a boss kill ends a dive in Trenchbore, collect the loose loot for the player.

## Grow the tree from the centre, and open screens only when they matter

### The upgrade tree

Nodebuster and Row Divers both use a large tree of nodes that you move around in. Nodebuster's tree **"unfolds to help foster the feeling of progression"** ([Steam guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3414691532)). It is "a giant node skill tree" with "6 different currencies" ([Higher Plain Games](https://higherplaingames.com/pc/nodebuster-review/)). Some nodes need one point in the node before them; others need the node before them to be maxed ([incrementaldb](https://www.incrementaldb.com/game/nodebuster)). Late in the game you must open 9 named branches, such as "to infinity", "void" and "no return", to reach the final areas ([Steam guides](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3107330/guides/)). One review complained that the auto-pickup upgrade costs "scale absurdly high", so it was the last thing bought ([incrementaldb review](https://www.incrementaldb.com/community/review/5808)). Press said it does "a great job of making every upgrade feel rewarding" and is "intuitive and well streamlined" ([The Mancunion](https://mancunion.com/2025/05/08/nodebuster-review-a-short-and-sweet-incremental-game/)). A Row Divers player wrote: **"I stayed for the beautiful skill tree"** ([Steam reviews](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2572300/reviews/?browsefilter=toprated)).

The complaints are about **moving around the tree and reading it**. A Nodebuster player could only reach deeper branches by zooming "in and out until it finally decides to move"; the answer was that you pan by holding the right mouse button ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3107330/discussions/0/4845401462970658556/)). A Row Divers player said the colours for "not enough resources, buyable, maxed" are "hard to differentiate" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198043284999/recommended/2572300/)). In To The Core, "being zoomed out doesn't let you click on some upgrade nodes" ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198027583924/recommended/1988550/)). Row Divers is also criticised because "most of the talents… are the same iterations of 'gain x health for y money'" ([Steam reviews](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2572300/reviews/?browsefilter=toprated)). Dome Keeper gets praise for the opposite: "it's obvious what you're getting and what it leads to in the progression tree" ([Josh Anthony](https://joshanthony.info/2023/05/24/design-dive-dome-keeper/)).

**About "?" nodes.** The famous "?" placeholder comes from Balatro, and it is used in a **collection, not an upgrade tree**. Each "?" comes with a hint: hovering shows "Not Discovered" and "an instruction on how to discover the item" ([Balatro Wiki](https://balatrowiki.org/w/Discoverability)). Other tree games show locked nodes as dark grey and the next buyable nodes in a clear colour ([Incremental Skill Tree](https://kingironfist101.itch.io/incremental-skill-tree)). Egg Tree Incremental keeps some upgrades fully hidden until you reach certain points ([Egg Tree Incremental wiki](https://egg-tree-incremental.fandom.com/wiki/Hidden_Upgrades)). So your dislike of "?" nodes has support. A whole field of "?" gives the player nothing to work with.

**About the look.** Nodebuster is described as having a "minimalist, sci-fi vibe" ([The Mancunion](https://mancunion.com/2025/05/08/nodebuster-review-a-short-and-sweet-incremental-game/)). None of the reference games needs a blueprint grid. A dark, clean background with glowing lines fits the genre and a deep-sea theme.

**About the tooltip.** No text source says where Nodebuster or Row Divers put their tooltip, or whether they show "before and after" values. That is a gap in the research. Based on Dome Keeper's praise for being "obvious what you're getting", my suggestion is a small card **next to the node you hover**, showing the name, the cost, and "now → next" values. A tooltip stuck in a screen corner makes your eyes jump away from the tree.

For Trenchbore's 81-node radial tree, the research points to this. Start with only the centre visible. Show bought nodes plus the **next ring only**, dimmed, with real names, costs and effects. Make the three states (can't afford, can buy, maxed) clearly different. Let players drag with the left mouse button or a touchpad, zoom with the scroll wheel, and press a "recenter" button. Put a few nodes in each branch that **change how you play**, not only "+x% damage". 81 nodes is not too many in itself; players "love big talent trees" ([Keep on Mining! review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198019025364/recommended/3769130/)). The problem is only when most nodes feel the same.

### "New" markers and screens that appear only when needed

The reference games **hide whole systems until the player needs them**. Nodebuster's Milestones tab is bought from the tree ([Nodebuster Bible](https://www.mejoress.com/the-nodebuster-bible-complete-walkthrough-skill-tree-and-prestige-strategy/)). Idle Slayer's Quests tab is bought with an upgrade, and daily quests are crafted ([Idle Slayer Wiki](https://idleslayer.fandom.com/wiki/Quests)). Mr. Mine opens systems at depth milestones ([Mr. Mine blog](https://blog.mrmine.com/mr-mine-idle-milestones/)). Some Vampire Survivors unlocks stay hidden until you meet a condition ([Vampire Survivors Wiki](https://vampire.survivors.wiki/w/Achievements)), and secret codes on the title screen unlock hidden characters as deliberate Easter eggs ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/vampire-survivors-best-secrets-unlocks/)). Players of small tree games talk about "hiding parts of the UI that aren't usable yet and gradually showing more content as players progress" ([itch.io feedback thread](https://itch.io/t/6102574/feedback)). Design writers call this "progressive disclosure", and say it is "not about hiding things indefinitely. It is about timing" ([Interaction Design Foundation](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/progressive-disclosure)).

For "new" markers, the warning is simple: **"If the badge is present most of the time, it stops being a signal and becomes wallpaper"** ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/beware-red-dot-badging)). A plain dot works best "when the fact that something changed matters more than the count" ([Setproduct](https://www.setproduct.com/blog/badge-ui-design)). In many games, red dots mean things like "unread mail", "claimable quest" and "new shop rotation" ([ui-reddot-system](https://github.com/kaan1altay/ui-reddot-system)). The research could not find how Nodebuster, Row Divers or Idle Slayer mark new things on screen.

For Trenchbore, my suggestions based on these sources are these. Show the Relics tab when the first relic is found. Show the Logbook when the first entry arrives. Show the Map after the first dive or first boss. When a tab appears for the first time, give it one short pulse or slide-in. Use a small calm dot for "new, not seen yet", and clear it the moment the player looks. Use a red dot with a number only for rewards the player can claim.

### The stats side panel

No source shows a permanent stats panel in any of the hit games. In Nodebuster, the space between runs is basically the tree: a run ends and you "take these Bits to the main screen and buy basic upgrades in the tech tree" ([Nodebuster Bible](https://www.mejoress.com/the-nodebuster-bible-complete-walkthrough-skill-tree-and-prestige-strategy/)). Even a results screen can annoy players; Digseum players asked to skip it ([review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198144574254/recommended/3361470/)). Ball x Pit gives you "one free round of harvesting" at your base after each run ([Maxi-Geek](https://www.maxi-geek.com/con/ball-x-pit-review)). Void Scrappers lets you "purchase permanent upgrades such as better attack and a more robust shield" after a run ([GameGrin](https://www.gamegrin.com/reviews/void-scrappers-review/)). A Game About Digging A Hole has no run screen at all: you go back to a garage terminal, sell ore, and buy upgrades ([GameGrin](https://www.gamegrin.com/reviews/a-game-about-digging-a-hole-review/)). Its upgrades are just four clear kinds: shovel, battery, inventory and jetpack ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/a-game-about-digging-a-hole-best-upgrades/)). So your dislike of the stats panel is well founded. A better choice is a **short results strip after each dive** (depth reached, loot, new log entries, any relic), which the player can skip, plus one big "Dive again" button. The research also found that replaying the same start again and again is "painful" ([Row Divers reviews](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2572300/reviews/?browsefilter=toprated)), so letting players start deeper once a zone is cleared is worth doing.

## Relics, the logbook and the map should feel like places to explore

Good collection screens **show what is missing, with a clue**. Balatro shows a "?" with a hint on how to find the item, and shows a silhouette for items that are unlocked but not yet found ([Balatro Wiki: Discoverability](https://balatrowiki.org/w/Discoverability); [Balatro Wiki: Collection](https://balatrowiki.org/w/Collection)). Ball x Pit shows a count of hidden blueprints on each level tile. But players complain that "Buildings don't appear in the Encyclopedia… making it hard to keep track of them", and that base building is "extremely clunky" ([Ball x Pit wiki](https://ballxpit.wiki.gg/wiki/Blueprints); [Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/ball-x-pit-base-layout-harvest-tips/); [Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2062430/discussions/0/624436409752623747/)).

Depth games **sort their world by depth**. Dave the Diver splits its fish into Shallows (0 to 50 m), Medium (50 to 100 m) and Depths (beyond 100 m, "where light struggles to come through") ([Pro Game Guides](https://progameguides.com/dave-the-diver/dave-the-diver-map-all-underwater-locations/); [fish locations](https://davethediver.blog/en/fish-locations/)). Mr. Mine uses km milestones, and relics are sacrificed at The Core at 501 km ([Mr. Mine blog](https://blog.mrmine.com/mr-mine-idle-milestones/); [blog index](https://blog.mrmine.com/)). In Dome Keeper's Relic Hunt mode, the relic is dug out and carried home ([Josh Anthony](https://joshanthony.info/2023/05/24/design-dive-dome-keeper/)), and the relic chamber is "usually located in the deepest layer of the map" ([Dome Keeper Wiki](https://domekeeper.wiki.gg/wiki/Relic_Hunt)). In Motherload, resources and dangers "became more dense on both ends as you descended" ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/reviews/review-super-motherload/)).

Two warnings. Super Motherload added bases as checkpoints, but "depths between outposts get longer, until by the end you're spending many minutes just to reach the site of the final boss" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/games/super_motherload/b/playstation4/archive/2013/11/22/digging-deep-shallow-play.aspx)). And Subnautica has **no map at all**. Players steer by landmarks, which feels real, but "it's easy to get lost and miss important landmarks" ([Robert Yang, Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/02/mapping-sea-floors-of-subnautica.html)). The designer's own talk on this choice was not read, so his reasons are unknown ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025745/The-Design-of-Subnautica)).

For Trenchbore, these are my suggestions built on the sources. Draw the map as a **side view (cross-section) of the trench** that gets darker as it goes down. Label each zone with its depth. Mark bosses and relic sites. Show counters per zone, such as "relics 2/4" or "log entries 5/9", like Ball x Pit. Let the map double as the screen where you choose a starting zone, so deep dives do not become long commutes. In the logbook, show unmet creatures as **dark silhouettes with a hint**, like "seen below 400 m in Zone 3", not a bare "?". Let relics be **found in the rock during a dive**, not bought in a menu, and give the first find a short freeze-frame and a sound. The research found no source that compares a drawn map with a list, so the side-view idea is a reasoned guess, not a tested fact.

## Suspense comes from hiding the goal, not from showing things slowly

You asked how these games keep suspense. The short answer: **they do not just show things slowly. They hide the goal, they tease with small clues, and they change what kind of game you are playing.** Three tools work together.

### Tool one: hide the goal and change the game

The famous incrementals start with almost nothing. Candy Box "starts with only a save button and a candy counter… options appear as the player performs actions", and it slowly becomes "a full role-playing adventure" ([Wikipedia: Candy Box!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candy_Box!)). Candy Box inspired A Dark Room ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/shedding-light-the-making-of-a-dark-room/)). In A Dark Room, you light a fire. A stranger arrives and becomes a builder. Later a shopkeeper sells a compass. Then the game turns into an adventure where "you explore a map, enter mini-dungeons, fight battles" ([TouchArcade](https://toucharcade.com/2014/06/03/a-dark-room-review/); [Jay is Games](https://jayisgames.com/review/a-dark-room.php)). A Dark Room "withholds the one piece of information that is traditionally the very first thing that is established… the object of the game". Its "central mystery… is what drives the player's desire to persist in what otherwise seems like simple spreadsheet management" ([PopMatters](https://www.popmatters.com/183319-a-dark-room-the-most-fun-youll-ever-have-with-a-spreadsheet-2495645232.html)).

Universal Paperclips moves through clear stages: clicking paperclips by hand, then machines and marketing, then AI upgrades, then the stock market, then drones turning Earth into clips, then space probes, then a final choice ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Paperclips); [IF50](https://if50.substack.com/p/2017-universal-paperclips)). Its second stage "is more akin to a power management simulator" ([Paperclips Wiki](https://universalpaperclips.fandom.com/wiki/Stages)). The genre calls these big changes **"paradigm shifts"**: "several phases of completely distinct gameplay that fully replace the previous" ([Wikipedia: Incremental game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_game)). One genre guide rates this "unfolding" as the most valuable design factor, because it creates "a sense of mystery, with the player anticipating what will happen next" and shakes "up the gameplay before it gets too stale" ([The Paper Pilot](https://paperpilot.dev/garden/guide-to-incrementals/defining-the-genre)). Kittens Game feels "like peeling back layers of an onion", and "allow[s] players to see forward through a chain of unlocks" ([malvasia bianca blog](https://malvasiabianca.org/archives/2018/08/kittens-game/); [Jay is Games](https://jayisgames.com/review/kittens-game.php)).

The small active games show the same thing. Players praise "new game play every ascension" ([Max Manos review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199811288100/recommended/3428620/)) and punish games where "the trailer show[s] everything" ([Row Divers review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199074593713/recommended/2572300/)).

### Tool two: tease with part of the thing, not all of it

The best teases are **partial**. Dome Keeper's director René Habermann said: "The single color shapes are best for this. It seems true that the best things happen in your mind", so they chose to "not visually 'overshare' the nature of the monsters" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-dome-keeper-focuses-on-systems-that-feed-into-one-another)). Its sound designer Martin Kvale said: "When you hear an ambience that you haven't heard before, you know there is something unknown on its way and that you may be in trouble" (same source). In Subnautica, players "often hear creatures long before they see them" ([The Geekwave](https://thegeekwave.com/2020/06/subnautica-and-sound/)). Many games show a place you can see but cannot reach yet, which "keeps the sense of wonder and curiosity alive" ([DualShockers](https://www.dualshockers.com/metroidvania-game-maps/)). Cookie Clicker slowly changes the tone of its text: around the 10th grandma, the upgrade text turns dark ("They are not your grandmas. They never were"), and then the whole screen changes in three stages ([Cookie Clicker Wiki](https://cookieclicker.wiki.gg/wiki/Grandmapocalypse); [Upgrades trivia](https://cookieclicker.wiki.gg/wiki/Upgrades/Trivia)). Inscryption's designer Daniel Mullins liked "the idea that you're seeing things that you weren't meant to see" ([Game Rant](https://gamerant.com/inscryption-interview-developer-daniel-mullins-3d-retro-horror-games/); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/-i-inscryption-s-i-journey-from-game-jam-joint-to-cult-classic)).

There is science behind this. Curiosity is the wish to fill a gap in what you know. But "when the knowledge gap is too large or too small, motivation to seek information declines" ([Psychology Fanatic](https://psychologyfanatic.com/information-gap-theory/); [Taylor & Francis study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10447318.2024.2325171)). Designers "can thus induce curiosity by creating or increasing the salience of information gaps" ([DiGRA](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/view/793)). This explains why a screen full of "?" nodes does not work: the gap is too big, with no clue to close it. One "?" with a hint does work.

A descent game has a special kind of fear. A study of deep-sea games says dread comes from being forced down: "you have to go down there, you can't tell if you're prepared, and there's no guarantee you'll make it back". Players get "just enough information to know that they should be cautious, but not enough to claim mastery" ([Game Studies, Monica Evans](https://gamestudies.org/2404/articles/evans)). Trenchbore's dive to hell fits this well.

### Tool three: mix steady rewards with surprise rewards

Vampire Survivors uses "multilayered rewards", so "no run ever feels wasted". Falling just short of the 30-minute mark creates a near-miss that makes you want "another run". Its designer had worked in the gambling industry ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/vampire-survivors-how-developers-used-gambling-psychology-to-create-a-bafta-winning-game-203613)). Inside a run, calm moments alternate with spikes of danger (same source). Talks on idle game design describe the "rush of progress" from a reset, and resets that unlock "entirely new content" ([GDC Vault 2016](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023876/Quest-for-Progress-The-Math); [GDC Vault 2015](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1022065); [slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/quest-for-progress-gdc-europe-2016/65405507)). Small developers report that "showing the next unlock's condition and subtle progress bars provide incentive to keep playing" ([itch.io thread](https://itch.io/post/15330988)); this is one comment, not hard data.

**Timing matters too.** In Subnautica, new distress beacons appear "every hour or so" in the first half, to push players to explore. Then this structure falls apart in the middle, and players lose direction ([Radiator Blog](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/02/mapping-sea-floors-of-subnautica.html)). Dave the Diver's director spent "a lot of time testing progression pacing and introducing unexpected events" ([Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/interviews/dave-the-divers-in-the-jungle-expansion-is-basically-dave-the-diver-1-7-says-director-even-players-who-usually-skip-dlc-should-give-it-a/); this is a paraphrase from a summary). No source gives a measured rhythm such as "a new thing every 2 minutes" for small incrementals. That number is unknown.

### What this means for Trenchbore

These are my own suggestions, built from the sources above. **Make each zone change the rules, not only the rock's toughness.** In one zone, light is scarce and your lamp becomes a resource. In another, the drill's noise draws hunters. In another, currents push the sub. At least once in the middle of the game, **change the main screen itself**, the way Paperclips did. **Keep one big mystery for the whole game**: why is this trench here, who dug it, and what is at the bottom? Tell it through logbook pages with missing words that fill in as you go deeper, and let the text slowly turn wrong, like Cookie Clicker's grandmas. **Tease each new zone with sound first**: on the dive before it opens, play its sound faintly at the bottom of the current zone. Show the next boss only as a shadow passing the window. On the map, draw explored zones fully, the next zone as a dark band with one hint ("a warm glow at 1,200 m"), and the rest as black. **Put surprise finds in the rock**, keep steady goals predictable, and show near-misses ("40 m from the next zone") when a dive ends.

Clones that copy a hit's look sell almost nothing, as the "Another Game About Digging A Hole" row shows.

### itch.io versus Steam for a $3 game

**itch.io is a good place to test and a poor place to earn.** Marketing expert Chris Zukowski says it plainly: **"You will not make money from Itch.io. Nobody does."** In his survey of 169 developers, the median itch page got **1,582 views in its whole life** and 113 downloads. Browser games get about 3 times more players than download-only games. He advises using itch as a 1 to 2 month test for whether a game has "the magic", then moving to Steam ([How To Market A Game](https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/05/12/benchmark-itch-io-traffic/)). itch.io gets tens of millions of visits a month; estimates range from about 44.5 million to 112 million, and about 41% arrive from search engines ([Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/website/itch.io/); [Exploding Topics](https://analytics.explodingtopics.com/website/itch.io); [Semrush](https://www.semrush.com/website/itch.io/overview/)). Its top-rated incremental games are mostly free browser games, such as Dig Dan Dig! and Idle Breakout, with a few paid ones like Tower Wizard at $2.99 ([itch.io top-rated incremental](https://itch.io/games/top-rated/tag-incremental)). But a big site does not mean your page gets seen. One developer shared that her whole itch catalogue earned $2,461.01 from 1,910 payments over several years ([Nathalie Lawhead](https://www.nathalielawhead.com/candybox/my-gross-revenue-on-itch-io-transparently-sharing-all-my-stats-earnings-and-speaking-on-how-supportive-of-a-base-itch-io-has)). A secondary guide says "most earn under $100 lifetime" on itch, but it gives no data source ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/web-game-monetization)).

On a $3 itch sale, itch takes 10% by default and the payment company takes $0.30 plus 2.9% ([itch.io docs](https://itch.io/docs/creators/payments)). **You keep about $2.31.** itch suggests a minimum price of $2 because of these fixed fees (same source). If a median page gets about 550 browser players, and 1% to 3% of them buy the full game, that is **about 5 to 17 sales, or roughly $12 to $40**. Even a page in the top 30% for views would give roughly $100 to $300. This is my estimate from the benchmark plus a rough buying rate for donations ([Generalist Programmer](https://generalistprogrammer.com/tutorials/how-to-make-money-on-itchio-indie-game-guide)); no one has measured demo-to-purchase rates on itch.

**The best example for your plan is Tower Wizard.** It sold on itch as a browser game for "$2.99 USD or more", with 781 ratings averaging 4.6 of 5 ([itch.io page](https://barribob.itch.io/tower-wizard)). The developer then moved it to Steam, where it earned an estimated **$0.5M to $0.9M gross** ([GameRevenueData](https://gamerevenuedata.com/games/tower-wizard/)). The real money came after the move.

Steam costs **$100 per game**, which you get back after the game earns $1,000 ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee)). You must wait 21 days after paying, and your "Coming Soon" page must be public for at least two weeks ([Steamworks onboarding](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/onboarding)). In the first week, games typically sell about **0.15 copies per wishlist** ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-state-of-steam-wishlist-conversions)). The median game in Steam Next Fest (a free demo festival) gains only **about 800 to 1,100 wishlists**, and games that release their demo months before the festival do about 2.5 times better ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/do-steam-demos-increase-sales); [How To Market A Game](https://howtomarketagame.com/benchmarks/)). Another source puts the median at about 15% for games with under 5,000 wishlists ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/how-many-wishlists-before-launch)); more detailed data on demo players exists but was not read closely ([Alinea Analytics](https://alineaanalytics.substack.com/p/wishlist-to-buyer-conversions-for)). Steam also helps players find look-alike games: its "More like this" list next to Nodebuster shows similar incrementals ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/recommended/morelike/app/3107330/)). Nodebuster also has YouTube videos and a Twitch category ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KsM950rUxsA); [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tdT5T4jkdtY); [Twitch](https://www.twitch.tv/directory/category/nodebuster)). No source says which channel sold the most copies of any of these games. A rough estimate for a modest Trenchbore launch: 1,000 to 2,000 wishlists, 150 to 400 first-week sales, about **$270 to $700 for you in week one**, with more sales over time.

| Path | Likely result for you (estimates, not promises) |
|---|---|
| itch.io only, normal traction | About 5 to 20 sales, about $10 to $50 |
| itch.io only, strong traction (a popular Reddit post) | About 50 to 400 sales, about $100 to $900 |
| Steam, modest (like Row Divers or Void Scrappers) | About 5k to 20k copies, about $9k to $35k |
| Steam hit (like Nodebuster or Digseum) | 250k+ copies, $0.7M+ gross. Rare. |

Your plan to put a free demo on one itch page and the paid game on a second page a week later has a hidden cost. **The second page starts from zero views.** One page with a playable browser demo and a "$2.99 or more" buy button, as Tower Wizard did, keeps all your visitors in one place. $3 is the right price; it is the most common price among the hits. **Price is not your problem; being found is.** Free web portals are not a hidden better option either, even if you wanted them. They pay from ads, roughly €1 to €2 per 1,000 plays, and ask you to build in their own ad and save code ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/web-game-monetization); [CrazyGames developer portal](https://developer.crazygames.com/); [Cinevva CrazyGames guide](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/publish-game-crazygames)). Your paid-only rule costs you nothing here.

You should also plan for **AI disclosure**. itch.io has a disclosure field with a "Code" category, and its staff said AI-written code counts ([itch.io update](https://itch.io/t/4309690/generative-ai-disclosure-tagging); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/ai/itchio-marketplace-now-requires-asset-creators-to-disclose-their-use-of-generative-ai-130031999.html)). Players can filter by these tags ([GamesMarket](https://www.gamesmarket.global/itchio-now-requires-gen-ai-content-to-be-tagged-in-all-games-and-asset-packs-711e824843d410eab51fcd9436836294/)), though some users say itch later made the labels less visible ([itch.io forum](https://itch.io/t/4527500/im-dismayed-that-itchio-decided-to-hide-which-gamesassets-are-ai-and-which-arent)). Steam's January 2026 form focuses on AI content that players see or hear, such as art and sound, not on coding tools ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/steam-updates-ai-disclosure-form-to-specify-that-its-focused-on-ai-generated-content-that-is-consumed-by-players-not-efficiency-tools-used-behind-the-scenes/); [TechPowerUp](https://www.techpowerup.com/345302/steam-ai-disclosure-gets-clarification-for-ai-in-dev-tools)). Your art is drawn by AI-written code and your sound is synthesized, which is a grey area; disclosing is the safer path. About 31% of games released on Steam in 2026 disclose AI, but they earn a smaller share of sales than their share of releases. Among AI games that failed, **72% used AI for visuals**. The study's summary: "AI made shipping a game dramatically cheaper. It did nothing to make anyone want to play it" ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/news/2026-07-20-steam-ai-disclosure-study)). AI-disclosed games have still grossed an estimated $660M on Steam in total, so disclosure does not stop sales ([Totally Human Media](https://www.totallyhuman.io/blog/games-with-ai-disclosures-have-grossed-an-estimated-660m-on-steam)). One survey says about 31% of players view AI use negatively and 8% would refuse to buy, but its original source could not be checked ([Tech Insider](https://tech-insider.org/steam-ai-disclosure-2026/)). Clean, deliberate code-drawn art is less likely to look like "AI slop" than image-generator art.

## Verdict: reshape Trenchbore, and build it for its last two hours

**My recommendation is to keep Trenchbore and reshape it heavily. Do not scrap it.** Here is why.

Its skeleton is the one players reward: short dives, a tree between dives, a deep theme, bosses, and a real ending. That is the Nodebuster and Digseum pattern. The drill-and-dive theme also has a built-in hook in Motherload nostalgia. If you scrap it and restart, you will most likely rebuild the same skeleton and meet the same risks again. The things you dislike are **surface problems**, and the research agrees with you on every one: "contracts" and empty achievements, "?" nodes, the corner tooltip, the blueprint grid and the stats panel. These are fixes, not reasons to restart.

But "keep" does not mean "keep as it is". The research says the danger for a game like this is **not the first hour. It is zones 6 to 9.** The dig games score lower than the arena games because their digging feels weightless, their deep layers repeat the early ones, and their endings are grinds. If Trenchbore's later zones are harder rock with new colours, and its tree is mostly "+x% damage", it will land in the 80% group, not the 95% group, and it will look like "just another nodelike". That is the real work of the reshape.

| Area | What to do |
|---|---|
| Goals | Replace "contracts" and empty achievements with a few themed goals (for example "Orders") that each pay something real and must be claimed. The list ends before the game does. |
| Damage | Cracks, hit-flash and debris on rocks. Short-lived bars only after a hit. One big bar with phase marks for bosses. A flashing and shake setting that truly works. |
| Upgrade tree | Grows from the centre. Only bought nodes plus the next ring, with real names. No "?" field. Clear colours for can't afford, can buy and maxed. Tooltip next to the node, with "now → next". Dark clean background, no grid. Left-drag to move, a recenter button. Some nodes in every branch that change how you play. |
| Screens | Remove the stats side panel. Add a short, skippable results strip and a big "Dive again" button. Open Relics, Logbook and Map only when first needed, with a one-time pulse. Calm dot for "new", red only for claimable. |
| Relics, logbook, map | Relics found in the rock during dives. Logbook with silhouettes and hints. Map as a darkening side view of the trench, with per-zone counters and a way to start deeper. |
| Suspense | Each zone changes a rule. One mid-game change to the main screen. One big mystery told through the logbook. New zones teased by sound, bosses by shadow. |
| Ending | Make the last hour the climax, not a grind wall and not a game that plays itself. Offer an optional endless dive after the ending. |
| Release | One itch page with a browser demo and a "$2.99 or more" full game. Treat it as a 1 to 2 month test. If players ask for more, open a Steam "Coming Soon" page with a demo and aim for Next Fest. Disclose AI use honestly. |

**What a really good paid game in this genre needs**, based on everything above, is this. It needs to be **3 to 5 hours long, priced at about $3 to $5**, and honest about its length on the store page. Players should never wait more than about 30 seconds for something to buy. The first upgrade should arrive after one short first dive and should be felt at once, for example fewer hits per rock. Early on, start weak in drill power and cargo, not in basic movement, because a slow start is called "painfully boring" ([Row Divers reviews](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2572300/reviews/?browsefilter=toprated)). Every zone needs **one new thing you can feel**: a material, a hazard, an enemy behaviour, or a rule. The player needs a few **"Oh yeah!" power moments**, like the Keep on Mining! lightning. There must be real choices, so that two players can build different subs. The game must not play itself late on, and costs must not jump into a wall. It needs to feel heavy and satisfying to drill, but with working comfort settings. It needs **no ads and no shop**, which matches your plan. It should run well and save reliably. And it needs **one idea players have not seen before**. That last one matters most in a market that grows fivefold in two years.

Be honest with yourself about money. If you sell only on itch.io, expect **tens to a few hundred dollars**, even if the game is good. Because an AI builds it, your cost is mostly time, so any sale is profit. The big results in this genre (Nodebuster, Tower Wizard, Digseum) happened on Steam, and they are the top of a very tall pile. A realistic good outcome for a polished Trenchbore on Steam is closer to Row Divers or Void Scrappers: **thousands to tens of thousands of dollars**. That is possible, but not likely, and only if the itch test shows that players truly want it.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. The question is not "is Trenchbore too small, or too big?" The hits are small, and players thank them for it. The real question is **"does the game keep surprising me until the end?"** The best games treat depth as a series of new rules. They treat collections as puzzles with clues. They treat the ending as a reward, not a leftover. In a game about diving into hell, the descent itself can be the suspense: a sound you have not heard, a shadow past the window, a log page with words missing. None of this needs more content than Trenchbore already has. It needs the content to be revealed with care.

The second lesson is that **money in this genre depends on where you sell more than on the price you set**. A $3 price is right. itch.io is the place to learn whether the game has "the magic", and Steam is the place where $3 games have earned real money. If the reshaped Trenchbore makes itch players ask "is this on Steam?", that is your signal to take the next step.
